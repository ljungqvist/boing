# Can Boing see the last foot leave?

**I would test visual takeoff first.** Keep the pose estimator to locate the jump and estimate its apex. Use the original video to estimate when the last supporting shoe left the floor. My first investment would be a blinded human reading of ordinary phone footage against a high-speed reference. At **60 fps**, a two-centimetre target on individual jumps looks plausible enough to test. At **30 fps**, meeting that target consistently would require information about *when within a frame interval* release occurred. Boing’s current evidence justifies that experiment, but does not establish accuracy at either rate.

## The foot landmark can move while the toe still supports weight

Boing usually fits a parabola to the tracked foot points and calls its crossing with the floor takeoff. As a jumper rolls onto their toes, the tracked point near the ball of the foot rises while the toe can still push against the floor. In one hand-marked 240 fps review, Boing estimated takeoff **17–30 ms early**. The two feet left the ground **2–50 ms apart** in that footage. Averaging their positions loses the distinction that matters for a two-foot jump. A fixed timing correction helped some sessions and overcorrected another. [Current estimator](../src/analysis/jumps.ts) · [240 fps review](../docs/evaluation/2026-10-15-accuracy-review.md) · [Offset experiments](../docs/accuracy-ideas.md)

Boing already has a promising pixel experiment. Across ten native 30 fps front-camera jumps from one person, a change in the appearance of the floor around the feet gave a mean signed height error of **+0.5 cm**, with a standard deviation of **2.5 cm** and a worst error of **3.6 cm**. The updated pose method on those ten jumps averaged **+1.7 cm**, with a standard deviation of **2.1 cm** and a worst error of **5.4 cm**. The pixel threshold was chosen on those jumps. The experiment also used reference times to narrow its search, changed both takeoff and landing, refitted the apex and scored a landing-capped height against flight-time height. The improvement in the mean cannot tell us how accurately it located takeoff. [Pixel results and pose baseline](../docs/accuracy-ideas.md) · [Experimental scoring](../tools/library/pixel_edges.py)

## A two-centimetre target leaves about eight milliseconds

For a 30 cm jump, takeoff to apex takes about 247 ms. From the ballistic relation *h = gT²/2*, calling takeoff 10 ms early adds **2.5 cm**. A ±2 cm target allows roughly **±8 ms of combined takeoff and apex timing error**. Suppose a reader perfectly identifies the last frame in contact and the next frame airborne, takes their midpoint, and the event occurs at random within that interval. With an exact apex, the 90th-percentile absolute height error is already about **3.6 cm at 30 fps** and **1.8 cm at 60 fps**. The image sequence could do better if the sole’s increasing clearance helps estimate the fraction of the frame. That is the hypothesis worth testing. [Jump-height mechanics](https://ebm.ufabc.edu.br/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/VerticalJump_Linthorne.pdf)

The proposed acceleration test is physically sound for the true whole-body centre of mass. As the ground force fades to zero, vertical acceleration approaches −9.81 m/s². The body can still be rising and slowing while the toe remains in contact. Near release, the supported trajectory and its airborne continuation are very close, and estimating acceleration from successive noisy positions magnifies the noise. A well-fitted airborne parabola can identify its apex without revealing where contact ended. Boing’s centre-of-mass curve fit improved takeoff on full-resolution 240 fps footage, but its native 30 and 60 fps trials gave worse height errors than the existing method. I would retire acceleration as the primary takeoff detector for these rates. ## One blinded replay would decide what to build

Start with the existing 240 fps clips. Use only jumps where both shoe–floor interfaces are visible to score last-foot release. On October 19 the far foot was hidden. Those frames can test whether a reader sees the near foot leave, while the last-foot reference remains uncertain. If the archive has too few clear jumps, record a short high-speed series showing both feet. Have two readers independently mark the **last certain contact** and **first certain clearance of both feet**. Show other readers the same footage sampled at 30 and 60 fps, hiding the reference, pose overlay and landing. Ask for the same bracket and allow “unreadable.” Vary the sampling phase and count each physical jump once. This cheap, same-clock comparison isolates frame rate. Then check the result on native phone recordings at the usual placement and exposure, across people, shoes, floors and lighting. A published test using silhouette pixels reported 20 ms mean absolute error in *flight duration* at simulated 30 fps. Equally late takeoff and landing can give the correct duration, so that result leaves our question open. [Chiu et al., methods and Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2025.112695)

For native footage, synchronize the reference independently and verify the timing uncertainty is comfortably below the target. Boing’s October 19 clocks were aligned using the app’s own flight midpoint with stated ±20 ms precision. That cannot resolve an 8 ms takeoff question. A synchronized force plate, if accessible for one session, gives release time and an independent height from takeoff velocity without needing landing. With high-speed video alone, label the conclusion as accuracy against *visible release*. [October 19 reference](../docs/evaluation/2026-10-20-side-view-reference.md) · [Clock construction](../tools/library/build.py) · [Force-plate reference method](https://ebm.ufabc.edu.br/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/VerticalJump_Linthorne.pdf)

On identical jumps, freeze Boing’s apex and compare **uncapped** rise using three takeoff times from the current pose, the blinded reader and the reference release. Score signed takeoff error, median and 90th-percentile absolute error in milliseconds, and the fraction of all jumps readable. The reference substitution tells us whether perfect takeoff would fix the height when independent height is available. If it still misses, investigate apex or camera geometry before investing in foot detection.

## What would earn a prototype?

My proposed decision threshold is **90% of measured jumps within 2 cm, with at least 90% of attempts measurable**, evaluated separately at each rate. This is an illustrative product target. If readers approach it, use pose to crop each foot at original resolution and measure sole-to-floor clearance across several frames near release. Take the later foot’s release and abstain when an occlusion or shadow leaves contact unresolved. On held-out people and sessions, let the detector find its own search window without reference timing and compare it with the midpoint and current pose. If only 60 fps passes, pursue that accuracy at 60 fps and establish a wider tolerance for 30 fps. If frequent blur or a hidden second foot defeats readers at both rates, try one practical change to capture conditions before investing in a detector. A small pilot can decide whether to proceed. A precision claim needs an independent set of native recordings.
